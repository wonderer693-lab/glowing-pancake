The marketing blur. "Heat pump" gets used to describe six different kinds of equipment. The actual choices are different in cost, install, and which houses they fit. Sort them once.

## Air-source split system

![Side-by-side diagram of a monoblock heat pump and a split heat pump, with the refrigerant line-set highlighted in the split version](/diagrams/monoblock-vs-split.svg)

The most common residential install in 2026. Outdoor condenser box. Indoor air handler or one or more wall-mounted heads. Refrigerant lines between them.

A ducted version puts the indoor coil inside a fan-driven air handler connected to your home's ductwork. A ductless version puts one or more wall-mounted heads in the rooms you want to condition. Often called a mini-split.

Most existing-house furnace swaps go ducted split. Most add-a-room or no-ductwork houses go ductless. Most builders in new construction in 2026 still default to gas furnace plus AC, which is changing but slowly.

## Mini-split (ducted or ductless)

A mini-split is one outdoor unit serving one to several indoor heads. The terminology is loose. A single-head ductless unit is a mini-split. A multi-head ductless unit is also a mini-split. A ducted mini-split with a small concealed air handler that feeds a few ceiling registers is also called a mini-split.

The useful distinctions. A single-zone unit has one indoor unit on one outdoor unit, the simplest. A multi-zone unit has two to five indoor heads running off one outdoor compressor, which is more efficient overall but distributes heating less precisely. A multi-zone unit normally modulates the compressor to match total demand, but each indoor head controls its own thermostat.

The classic mistake with multi-zone. You pick a 4-zone outdoor unit, then run one head at a time, and the compressor spends its evening loafing. Better efficiency comes from matching the number of zones to actual simultaneous use. If two heads are usually on together, get a 2-zone system, not a 4-zone that you half-use.

## Packaged heat pump

Single cabinet, usually on a roof or beside the building. All four moving parts (compressor, indoor coil, outdoor coil, fan) in one box. Conditioned air is delivered through a short duct.

Common in commercial properties, manufactured homes, and south-attached dwellings where ductwork is concentrated. Not common in single-family residential new installs in 2026 because the units historically lagged in efficiency. New packaged units have caught up, with SEER2 16 to 19 ratings, but contractors rarely recommend them.

## Air-to-water (hydronic)

Outdoor unit pumps refrigerant indoors to a heat exchanger that transfers heat to water. The water then runs through underfloor loops, radiators, or a fan-coil convector. Same architecture as a boiler with a heat pump replacing the boiler.

Common in northern Europe. Pole-position in Netherlands, Germany, Scandinavian retrofits. Less common in the US because few installers know the work. The US is catching up under IRA pressure, with brands like Chiltrix, SpacePak, Arctic, and Mitsubishi EcoDan bringing products to market.

Most existing-house radiator retrofits use this architecture. See the heat-pump-vs-boiler guide for the full retrofit picture.

## Ground-source (geothermal)

Outdoor coil exchanged for a buried loop of antifreeze. Loop is buried 1 to 2 meters down (horizontal) or 50 to 100 meters down (vertical wells). Stable ground temperature of 8 to 12C year round, so the compressor never sees a -25C morning.

The ground loop is the install cost multiplier. See the air-source-vs-ground-source guide for the comparison math. In 2026 cold-climate air-source units closed most of the COP gap, so ground-source is now mostly a niche option for new builds where the well-drilling rig is already on site.

## Window heat pump

A real category now. Midea, Windmill, and a few others ship window-sized heat pumps that fit a single-hung window opening. The blades are smaller, the compressor is smaller, the COP is lower (around 2 to 2.5 in shoulder season). For a small room in a cold climate, this is a $400 to $700 stopgap that lets you skip a whole-house install.

They handle one room. They do not handle a whole house. They have their own defrost cycles, can be loud, and connect via a standard plug. Renter-friendly, splits between tenants,window-blanket noise. Worth considering as the first heat pump in an apartment, less worth considering as the last upgrade in a house.

## Variable capacity refrigerant flow (VRF)

Mostly commercial but worth knowing. A VRF system runs many indoor heads from one outdoor unit with refrigerant metering at each head. The compressor modulates down to 5 percent of nameplate capacity, which scales efficiency at part-load. Residential equivalents are sometimes called "Multi F" (LG) or "Multi-Zone" (Mitsubishi).

At residential scale a true VRF unit is overkill. The best residential inverter multi-zone units already modulate down to 25 or 30 percent of capacity, which is close enough for residential load profiles.

## The one piece of advice that applies to every type

Every type on this list works. The decision is not which architecture is best, it is which architecture matches your house and installer. The installer matters more than the brand matters more than the architecture. Get a contractor whose last five installs were the architecture you are considering, not one whose last five were something else and who is now "available to try this too."
