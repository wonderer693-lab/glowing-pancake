A heat pump install quote has five or six line items. Hardware. Labor. Line-set and refrigerant. Electrical. Permitting and inspection. Plus a usually unbudgeted category called "ancillary," which is everything that the contractor finds mid-way through and writes on a follow-up invoice.

## Hardware

Outdoor unit, indoor unit or air handler, optional electric resistance backup strip, optional dedicated thermostat. For a 3-ton cold-climate multi-zone install, this lands around $6,000 to $9,500 depending on brand and tier. Mitsubishi HyperHeat sits at the top, Daikin Aurora near, and Bosch IDS HVS at value. LG Multi F Red slots between Mitsubishi and Bosch, depending on local service network.

The hardware is a commodity item. Three installers quote the same Mitsubishi H-Code. The difference in quote is rarely the hardware. The difference is labor and extras.

## Line-set and refrigerant

Copper line-set from outdoor unit to indoor head, plus the refrigerant charge to fill it. Line-set runs $4 to $9 per foot installed and the run on a 3-ton split system install averages 25 to 40 feet per zone. Added up across two to three zones, $300 to $1,200 per project.

The vacuum pump-down and system charge run $250 to $450 in labor time. Tests for leak rate by pressure drop usually take 30 to 60 minutes. If the contractor tries to start the system before the vacuum reading hit the magic microns number, the system enters service with moisture and eats the compressor over years. Insist on a written vacuum record.

## Electrical

Three serious items.

A dedicated 240V 30A to 50A circuit at the outdoor unit, includes wiring and breaker at the panel. $400 to $900 typical.

If your electrical panel is full, if the panel rating is below 200A, or if you want to integrate a future EV charger, expect the panel upgrade quote to run $2,500 to $5,000. This is one of the most common surprises.

If the existing service drop is overhead and the mast utility ownership is unclear, you may need a service upgrade from the meter base inwards. Add $1,000 to $2,500. Most heat pump installs of pre-1990 houses hit this.

## Permitting and inspection

$100 to $500 permit fees in most US regions. The electric permit and the HVAC permit are usually separate. A good contractor bundles.

The electrical inspector checks the dedicated circuit. The HVAC inspector checks line-set vacuum and refrigerant charge. The energy code inspection might check AFUE/HSPF2 rating, depending on local code cycles.

Most cold-climate state rebates require an inspector signoff. Without the permit you can forfeit the rebate. Ask the contractor about the permit before the install starts. Some contractors skip permitting for speed reasons; you take the rebate risk.

## Labor

A 3-ton heat pump install in cold climate takes 1.5 to 3 days depending on complexity. At cold-climate cold-market labor rates, that is 16 to 32 labor hours. At $85 to $140 per labor hour with overhead, labor lands $1,400 to $4,500 per project.

The labor difference between a $14k Mitsubishi install and a $20k Mitsubishi install is mostly time. Line-set routing in crawlspaces, ductwork modifications, panel work, condensate line routing, custom bracketry, air sealing. A two-day install costs $2,500 in labor; a three-day install costs $4,500.

## Ancillary: the surprise extras

Condensate pump for indoor heads in basements or interior walls: $80 to $200.

Pad for the outdoor unit (concrete 24"x24" or composite pad stakes): $75 to $250.

Insulated suction accumulator on long line-sets over 50 feet: $80 to $200.

Defrost drain pan heater for indoor heads in colder than -25C climates: $60 to $150.

Winter cover for outdoor unit: $25 to $80. Manufacturer-approved only; ad-hoc covers trap condensation.

Helper carpentry: blocking, drywall repair, paint touch-up. $200 to $800 depending on what the crew tears up.

Average ancillary line per install: $500 to $1,500. Usually not in the first quote, sometimes in the second quote once the contractor walks the site.

## An example quote, fully transparent

A 3-ton cold-climate Mitsubishi HyperHeat multi-zone install in central Massachusetts, kitchen and primary bedroom zones, existing 200A panel, line-set 35 foot run average, permits in Boston metro:

- Hardware (1 outdoor 36k + 2 indoor heads): $7,200
- Line-set ($6 / ft × 70 ft, brazing and charge): $540
- Electrical (240V 40A dedicated circuit, no panel upgrade): $550
- Permitting (HVAC + Electrical, Boston metro): $300
- Labor (2.5 days, full crew): $3,400
- Ancillary (condensate pump, ceiling bracket, pad, blocking): $400
- Total pre-rebate: $12,390

After $2,000 federal credit and $4,800 Mass Save Moderate Income rebate: $5,590.

A similarly-specced quote from a lower-tier installer, no panel work needed, line-set shorter: $11,000 installed, net $4,200 after rebates.

The same project from the high-end Diamond installer including a heat-loss survey, hot water desuperheater, balancing dampers on the ductwork: $16,800 installed net $10,000 after rebates.

Different quotes, different scopes. Read line items. Ask what is missing from any quote before signing. The surprise extras always come in after signature if not bundled upfront.

## Three lines to remember when reviewing quotes

The hardware is not what makes the quote move. Labor time and ancillary costs do.

A panel upgrade pile is the surprise $2,500 that traps unwary pre-1980 homeowners with 100A service panels. Get the panel rating before signing.

A cold-climate rebate typically requires a building permit and a green-sheet inspection signoff. Skipping the permit to save $300 on the price forfeits the $4,000 to $10,000 rebate. The math only makes sense if the rebate does not exist.
