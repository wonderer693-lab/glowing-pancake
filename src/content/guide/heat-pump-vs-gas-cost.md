The intersection of "should I switch" with "is it cheaper to run" comes down to three numbers in your specific zip code. Three formulas. One comparison.

The numbers are your local gas rate (in $/therm), your local electric rate (in $/kWh), and your heat pump's seasonal COP (roughly 2.4 for cold-climate, 3.0 for milder). Substitute them below and the answer drops out.

## Cost per million BTU comparison

For gas furnaces:

`Gas cost per million BTU = (gas price per therm) ÷ (0.095)`

The 0.095 factor comes from 100,000 BTU per therm times 95 percent furnace efficiency to get 95,000 BTU per therm delivered. For older furnaces at 80% AFUE use 0.080.

If your local gas price is $1.40 per therm, gas cost is $14.74 per million BTU delivered.

For heat pumps:

`Heat pump cost per million BTU = (electric price per kWh) × 293 ÷ (seasonal COP)`

The 293 factor converts kWh to BTU (1 kWh = 3,412 BTU, 1 million BTU = 293 kWh). The seasonal COP adjustment accounts for inverter modulation across the heating season.

If your electric is $0.20 per kWh and seasonal COP is 2.6, heat pump cost is $0.20 × 293 ÷ 2.6 = $22.54 per million BTU.

In this example, gas is cheaper at the rates given. That's true for most US gas-rich states at 2026 rates.

In strictly running cost terms, the heat pump wins or ties in places where:
- Electricity is unusually cheap (Washington state hydro, Quebec hydro, nuclear-heavy Ontario)
- Gas supply does not run to the street (Maine, Vermont, lots of New England and highland Cascadia)
- Climate is moderate enough for a COP of 3.0 to be a realistic seasonal average

The heat pump loses to gas in pure winter operation in most of the US Midwest, the South, the Maritimes coast, and anywhere prices are still disconnected.

## Then there is the dual-fuel case

If you have a furnace still in service, the dual-fuel option lets you switch fuels week by week. The control board reads outdoor temperature and chooses the cheaper option each cycle based on a switchover setting.

A common dual-fuel setup runs the heat pump down to about -7C outdoor and switches to the gas furnace colder. Operating cost over a winter averages 60 to 80 percent heat pump and 20 to 40 percent gas. Annual cost lands 25 to 40 percent below the same winter run on gas alone.

This is what most cold US installs end up looking like. Run whichever is cheaper per winter week.

## Reasons to switch even when gas is cheaper

Two reasons.

First. Emissions matter. A 95 percent AFUE furnace releases 117 pounds of CO2 per therm burned. A heat pump running on US grid average electricity releases 0.99 pounds per kWh consumed at COP 3.1. That is 32 pounds delivered per million BTU from a heat pump vs 117 pounds from a gas furnace. Triple cleaner.

Second. Gas is going to get more expensive. Natural gas prices in the US have risen 35 percent since 2021. Renewable electricity prices have fallen. The decade-out path is heat pumps becoming more favorable than gas even on raw running cost in most states.

If you have a 50+ percent solar offset on your electricity bill, the heat pump operating cost becomes competitive even in expensive-electric states. Solar plus heat pump resets the math completely in favor of electric.

## Reason to not switch

Pure dollar math. In an existing house with a working 95% AFUE gas furnace and rate plan that keeps electricity expensive relative to gas, swapping to a heat pump will probably not pay back in the unit's lifespan.

The math doesn't favor a switch in California under PG&E, parts of New York City under Con Ed, or Colorado under Xcel as 2026 rates stand today. Some of this changes when the rate plan does. Always compute your own case with the formula above.

## Three lines to remember

Gas furnace operating cost swings with the commodity market. Heat pump operating cost swings with COP. The two slide in opposite directions and the crossover moves from year to year.

Federal and state rebates almost never award you for installing a gas furnace. Most rebate programs award heat pump installs. Add that $2,000 to $10,000 to the operating cost comparison before deciding the unit makes no sense.

The right question is not "is the heat pump cheaper than the furnace at today's rates." It is "Is the heat pump cheaper than the furnace at the rates expected in years 5 to 15 of the unit's life." That answer is Yes in a growing list of states, the same in fewer states, and No in a shrinking list.
