The outdoor unit wants three things. Adequate airflow without re-circulation. Some shade from direct sun in summer. A mounted surface that does not transmit vibration into living areas. Most installs violate one or two of these. The cost of violation is real.

## Airflow and clearance

The condenser fan pulls outdoor air across the coil from the sides and dumps exhaust air out the top. Two clearance numbers matter.

Side clearance. 12 inches minimum from any wall, fence, or shrub. Tighter than that and the fan pulls its own exhaust back through. Re-circulation raises the effective outdoor temperature into the coil by 2 to 5 C and drops COP proportionally.

Top clearance. 5 feet minimum recommended. Above a deck railing, away from a soffit. A soffit 18 inches above the unit throws the unit's exhaust back into itself, drops cold-climate output 15 to 20 percent on design day.

For cases where the side-yard is too tight, installers sometimes use a "slim-line" unit or mount the outdoor unit on the roof. Roof mounted installs are more efficient in some shaded urban situations and are priced $1,500 to $2,500 higher due to extra rigging.

## Sun and shade

The outdoor unit wants no direct radiant heat in summer on the coil. In winter, direct sun barely matters; the air is what controls COP. In summer cooling operation, sun on the coil raises the effective condenser temperature by 3 to 5 C and drops cooling efficiency 6 to 10 percent.

The right shading is a trellis, a fence, or an offset roof. Do not box the unit in. If the shading blocks airflow, the loss is worse than the gain. A simple side-trellis 3 to 4 feet away with a climbing vine is the classic New England answer.

Avoid dense shrubs. Shrubs less than 24 inches away trap humidity and clog the coil with debris.

## Vibration and mounting

The compressor is a mechanical pump. It walks at 25 to 50 dB. Handled wrong, it transmits feeling into the floor above.

Three mounting surfaces commonly used:

- Concrete pad at grade: gold standard. Mass absorbs high-frequency vibrations naturally.
- Composite pad stakes: acceptable. At 25 to 35 dB at 1 meter, similar decibel reading to concrete.
- Wall-mounted or platform-roof mounted: loudest. The wall mount or platform shares vibrations with the wall or roof deck. Use 4 to 6 isolation pads rated for the unit's weight. Without pads, expect to hear low-frequency thrumming in any room above and below adjacent to the unit.

Do not mount equipment onto the siding or under a bedroom window. The vibration moves into the floor into the wall into the room. Best location is opposite the bedroom wall with the unit on concrete.

## Proximity to bedrooms and neighbors

Most municipal codes specify 5 feet from the property line and 8 feet from neighbor windows for outdoor condenser units. Check yours before placing.

Distance from bedrooms:

- 0 to 5 feet from exterior bedroom wall: you will hear the unit at low fan 25 dB. Depending on the wall, may bother sound-sensitive sleepers.
- 6 to 12 feet: probably quiet unless your walls are thin.
- 13+ feet: typically inaudible in the bedroom.

For multi-family or shared-wall housing, the placement choice matters more than the brand choice. Talk to your installer; ask them to walk the placement with you before the truck arrives.

## Line-set routing

Refrigerant line-set goes between outdoor unit and indoor head or air handler. Manufacturer limit on run length is typically 50 feet for residential single-zone, 75 to 100 feet for some multi-zone units. Beyond the rating, the warranty gets voided.

Two reasons to keep line-set short.

First. Cost. Each extra foot of line-set runs $4 to $9 installed. Copper is not the cost killer, the labor is. A 30-foot line-set with two bends takes an hour to braze. A 60-foot run around the house can take half a day, add $500 to install cost.

Second. Efficiency. Each 90-degree bend adds 5 percent to the equivalent length pressure drop. Long line-sets need additional refrigerant charge and weigh on the compressor. The manufacturer publishes a line-set length effect on capacity table at the back of the install instructions. READ it before signing the contract.

## Defrost drain management

The outdoor coil freezes in winter. Defrost runs every 30 to 90 minutes depending on humidity and temperature. The defrost cycle melts frost. The meltwater has to go somewhere.

- Poured concrete pad with a small drain pan raise above grade: best.
- Ground-level pan connected to a drain or splash block: standard.
- No defrost management: water pools around the pad and re-freezes. Ice forms under and around the unit. Eventually the pan thermistor detects ice and shuts the unit down at the worst moment.

The good installer routes defrost meltwater away from the pad. Cuts a small trench to a splash block at least 6 feet away. Adds a pan heater if your climate dips below -15C regularly so the defrost water does not re-freeze on contact with the pan bottom.

## The single most expensive mistake

Most expensive mistake: the unit is placed against the side of the house, less than 12 inches of side clearance, full sun on south-facing wall, on a wall bracket shared with the bedroom above.

The unit works, but it loses 15 to 25 percent of spec COP. The homeowner complains. The installer blames the manufacturer. The warranty exchange gets declined because the unit works "to spec" given the conditions.

Walk the install location with the contractor before signing. Use the spec sheet's clearance table. Ask about the side yard, the wall distance, the shading, the wall mount. If you do not get answers, find another installer.
