Ground-source heat pumps were the obvious cold-climate answer for thirty years. The ground is a stable 8C to 12C all year. Pulling heat from 10C is easy. Pulling heat from -15C air puts a compressor through more stress than it can take.

Then inverter vapor injection happened. Air-source units caught up so fast that 2026 ground-source installs are mostly for the people who already committed to the brand, not for the people running the cost numbers fresh. Most rebates favor either option, which flattens the case further.

## What ground-source is, mechanically

A long loop of plastic pipe buried in your yard. Filled with antifreeze. The loop runs from the indoor unit out, through trenches or vertical wells, and back. Heat is exchanged with the soil instead of the air. There is no outdoor fan. The unit is quieter than a split air-source unit, which some neighborhoods care about.

The loop is the cost. A shallow horizontal loop needs a real piece of land, say 400 square meters of access for drilling and trenching, with soil type that takes a backhoe. A vertical loop needs 50 to 100 meter bore holes, drilled by a rig that rents at $1 per foot per hole for the boring crew alone. The buried fluid is forever. The buried labor is forever. The cost is up front, all of it, on day one.

## The efficiency comparison

Ground-source COP lands around 4 to 5 at design temperature. Air-source cold-climate units land around 1.8 to 2.5 at -15C in real field tests, around 3 to 4 at shoulder season 8C.

So ground-source runs at higher COP in the worst stretch of winter. The argument stops there in favor of ground source, until you look at the install cost and operational savings.

## The cost comparison, with numbers

A straight air-source cold-climate install for a 2,000-square-foot house in New England in 2026 lands around $16k installed, before any rebates. After a $2,000 federal credit and a state rebate of $1,500, total is about $12,500.

A ground-source install for the same house lands around $38k installed. The same $2,000 federal credit applies, and some state programs add $5,000 to $10,000 more. Net is around $26k to $31k.

The ground-source unit saves about 1,500 kWh per winter in cold climates versus an air-source cold-climate unit. At US average blended electric of 17 cents per kWh, that is $255 per year. Payback on the $13,000 to $18,000 difference is 50 to 70 years.

The math has not changed much in five years. What has changed is that air-source cold-climate units now exist with COP above 2 down to -15C. In 2018 that did not exist outside lab tests.

## When ground source still wins

A handful of cases still tip the math toward ground source. You live on an acreage with poor air-source siting and the neighbor would object to an outdoor fan. You are building a new house, you already need well drilling, and the same rig can bore the loop while it is on site. You are in a climate with severe cold snaps below -25C where cold-climate air-source begins to depend on resistive backup heavily.

Otherwise, in 2026, the betting money is on a cold-climate air-source unit. The money saved on install can fund the next twenty winters of operation, and the difference in COP at peak heating hours is not enough to flip the math in most rate plans.

## One more honest note

The people selling ground-source units talk about heat pumps you bury and forget. The people selling air-source units talk about how the new inverter compressors change everything. Both are partly right. Both are partly selling.

The clean rule. Get three quotes. Make at least one air-source and at least one ground-source. Ask each contractor what they would install if they were paying. That answer is usually better information than the proposal itself.
