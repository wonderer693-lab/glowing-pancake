A heat pump and a furnace both keep your house warm. The room does not know or care which one is doing the work. The differences are in cost, in fuel, in comfort, in what happens when it breaks.

The first decision is fuel. A gas furnace burns methane, with propane and oil as alternatives depending on what runs to the street. A heat pump runs on whatever generated your electricity. In most of the US electricity still has gas and coal in the mix, but the per-unit emissions from running a heat pump at COP 3 are a small fraction of the per-unit emissions from burning gas at 95% AFUE.

If your electricity is mostly nuclear or hydro, this is a one-sided argument. In Quebec, Norway, France, Washington state, your emissions are basically zero running a heat pump. If your grid is heavy coal (West Virginia, parts of Poland, much of India) the picture is more nuanced. The emissions from running a COP 2.5 heat pump on a coal grid still barely beat a 95% gas furnace, but not by much.

## Running cost, the comparison that actually matters

Take a 2,000-square-foot well-insulated house in a cold climate. Annual heat load around 60 million BTU. Compare three setups.

A 95% AFUE gas furnace at $1.40 per therm. About 63 therms input per million BTU delivered after efficiency, so about $88 per million BTU. Annual cost roughly $5,300. Cut insulation improvements and you cut that number, but for the comparison the base is the base.

A heat pump at seasonal COP 2.6 running on electricity at $0.20 per kWh. At COP 2.6, 1 kWh delivers 2.6 kWh of heat, which is 8,860 BTU. Need about 113 kWh per million BTU, multiplied by 60 million BTU for 6,780 kWh per year. Annual cost $1,356.

A dual-fuel setup running the heat pump down to -7C switchover and the gas furnace only on colder days. Annual cost around $1,850, blending the cheaper of the two fuels week by week.

## Comfort differences

A furnace delivers air in the 49C to 54C range. Air at a register feels warm to the touch.

A heat pump delivers air in the 32C to 38C range. Air at a register feels lukewarm to the touch, almost cool if you stand over it.

It takes about two weeks for homeowners to stop noticing. The room keeps the same temperature as the furnace kept it. The feel of the air is different, less dramatic, slower. The complaint from furnace migrants is always the same. "The vents feel cold." The room is not cold. The vents are.

The defrost cycle, which happens every hour or two when it is humid and cold out, interrupts the heat pump for 5 to 10 minutes. The indoor air handler still blows, but the supplied air drops to room temperature for that stretch. Some units fire resistive strips during defrost to cover the gap. Better units stage the backup rarely.

## Failure modes

A furnace fails in a clear binary way. The igniter breaks, the gas valve sticks, the fan motor burns. The house goes cold within hours because there is no heat.

A heat pump fails softly until it fails hard. The COP drops 30% due to a slow refrigerant leak and you do not notice until the bill arrives. The defrost logic glitches and you get cold spurts every 45 minutes for a winter before someone fixes it. The reversing valve seizes and the unit still heats, but cannot switch to cooling in summer.

Furnaces are easier to repair. Heat pumps require a tech with manifold gauges, a charging chart from the manufacturer, and the willingness to read it. Many techs do not. Get a referral before signing.

## What I would actually choose

In 2026, for most US and UK climates with reasonable electric rates, the heat pump wins. For deep cold climates below -25C, dual fuel is the safer play. The furnace runs ten nights a winter; the heat pump runs the other 355. If your grid is dirty coal, hold a furnace for emissions reasons unless you can also buy solar or a renewable energy plan.

The decision is historical. The heat pump used to be a climate-and-emissions tradeoff. Now most of the math lands on heat pump side even at the dollar register. The furnace is not gone but the days of the furnace being the obvious default are.
