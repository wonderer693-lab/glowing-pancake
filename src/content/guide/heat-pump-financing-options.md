Heat pump installs are $10k to $25k. Most homeowners finance at least part. Four financing paths are real and worth comparing.

## Zero-interest state heat loan programs

Mass Save Heat Loan. Zero percent up to $25,000 for 7 years for Massachusetts residents. Covers cold-climate heat pump installs. Initiated through participating lenders, the most common being the Mass Save partner program through local banks including East Boston Savings and Cambridge Savings.

Vermont Heat Saver Loan. Zero percent through VSECU for cold-climate heat pump installs, up to $20,000 for 7 years. Similar in scope.

Maine Home Energy Loan Program. Low 5.99 percent over 10 years but not zero. Massachusetts, Connecticut, New Hampshire, and Wisconsin have similar programs.

The state programs go through participating local lenders. The lender submits the loan application and the state energy office subsidizes the rate. The borrower fronts a credit check.

Documentation: An installer quote on letterhead, pre-approval through the lender's portal, post-install verification through the state program before disbursement.

## Utility on-bill financing

Many utilities roll the loan onto your monthly electricity bill. The utility manages collections. Programs typically zero percent or low interest (below 3 percent).

Examples:
- Pacific Gas & Electric through the California Energy Loan
- PSE&G On-Bill Investment (currently zero percent plus slightly positive offset for heat pump install)
- Puget Sound Energy Field Demonstration Program
- Energy Trust of Oregon Heat Pump Loan

On-bill programs close with the post-install inspection. They typically run 5 to 10 years. The finance charge, if any, is folded into the monthly bill.

Disadvantage. The loan rides with the property if you sell. Some buyers do not like that; new buyers take the equipment debt.

## Federal tax credit stacking with financing

Apply for financing first, install the equipment, pay the lender through the loan disbursement, then claim the federal tax credit on your tax return for that year. The credit does not pay down the loan; it returns to your bank in the tax refund.

Common practice. Use the tax credit refund to pay down part of the loan in the following year. Reduces total loan cost without committing your full bank balance to the install.

## Home equity line of credit (HELOC)

For homeowners with sufficient equity, a HELOC at a rate around 7.5 to 8.5 percent may make sense if state loan programs do not run in your area.

Risks. The loan is secured by the property, so any default issues can lead to foreclosure. The rate is variable. Prime has risen from 3.25 percent in early 2022 to 8.5 percent in late 2025. Some forecasts say prime falls back to 5 to 6 percent by late 2027; some say otherwise.

Worth using HELOC if:
- Your state has no zero percent heat pump program
- You have enough equity to access $20k easily
- You will pay back the HELOC within 5 to 7 years

## Personal loan (unsecured)

Personal loans run 7 to 12 percent as of mid 2026. Higher than HELOC. Higher than state zero percent. Faster to access; can disburse in a week.

Pricing differs widely between lenders. Compare APR after origination fees. SoFi and LightStream have unsecured personal loan products that process heat pump installs under home improvement categories.

Unsecured loan has no recourse beyond a credit score; default lands as an unpaid trade line on credit reports. Lower risk to the lender technically, so the rate is higher.

## Caveats to the zero percent state loans

Watch the closing fees. Some state zero percent programs charge an origination fee of $100 to $300. That's small relative to the install but worth confirming.

Late fees apply after 30 days. Some programs revert to a market interest rate after 60 days late or after third late payment. Read the fine print on the loan agreement before signing.

Income documentation is required. Two years of W-2 plus two months of bank statements is typical. Self-employed present Schedule C plus year-to-date profit and loss.

## What I would do in 2026 with my own money

For a Massachusetts household at moderate income.

- Sign Mass Save Heat Loan for $20k at zero percent for 7 years. Monthly payment $238.
- Apply for Mass Save Moderate Income rebate. Receive $7,500 back from Mass Save within 60 days post-install.
- Apply for federal $2,000 tax credit on April 15 of following year.
- Total cost of install: $16,500 list - $7,500 Mass Save - $2,000 federal = $7,000 net cost.
- Monthly loan payment $238 × 84 months = $20,000 paid back to bank; volume paid net of rebates = $7,000 over 7 years = average $83 per month.

Seven years of heating plus cooling for $83 a month net. That is real and the same math applies in most states with active IRA rebate programs. The bankrolling of the upfront cost makes the heat pump ecosystem genuinely accessible to people who do not have $20,000 in cash sitting in their checking account at the moment they want to buy one.
