A heat pump is a refrigerator that can run backwards. That is the whole mechanical idea. The compressor shuffles heat from outdoors to indoors in winter, and reverses to shuffle heat from indoors to outdoors in summer. The electricity runs the compressor and fans, not a glowing wire or a flame.

If you have ever owned a fridge, you have lived next to one. The cold side faces inward and food keeps. The warm side faces the coil on the back of the fridge and dumps heat into your kitchen. Reverse the direction of refrigerant flow, mount the cold coil outdoors and the warm coil indoors, and you have a heat pump warming your house in January.

Three things trip up homeowners when they start shopping.

## Heat pumps do not "create" heat

They move it. Even at -20C the outside air holds heat energy. The refrigerant in a cold-climate unit has a boiling point below -30C. Outside air warms it enough to evaporate even on cold days. The compressor then squeezes that vapor warm, raises its pressure, and condenses it indoors. The "heat" came from outside air plus the work the compressor did.

This is why the efficiency numbers look impossible to people used to furnaces. A 95% efficient gas furnace gives you 0.95 units of heat for every unit of fuel. A heat pump at a COP of 3 gives you 3 units of heat for every unit of electricity. It is not violating physics. Two of those three units came from outside air.

## "Heat pump" usually means "split-system air-source"

The term covers a few different architectures. Most residential installs in the US and UK are air-source split systems. One outdoor box, one or more indoor heads or a coil in the ductwork.

Ground-source (geothermal) units use a buried loop instead of outdoor air. They are more efficient but cost two to four times more. They are not in fashion for new installs in 2026 because cold-climate air-source units got good enough to spank ground-source on bang for buck.

Water-source is a third kind, mostly commercial.

If someone says "heat pump" without qualifying, they mean air-source. This site defaults to that meaning too.

## A single unit can heat and cool

This is the part that lets HVAC salespeople quote the same box twice. A split-system heat pump with an indoor coil replaces both a furnace and an AC. If your old setup was a gas furnace plus a condenser on the roof, you can rip both out and slip one outdoor unit in.

In cold climates the unit is usually paired with backup heat. Either resistive electric strips inside the indoor air handler, or a small gas furnace in what is called a dual-fuel setup. Backup is for the coldest stretch of winter when the heat pump alone cannot carry the load. Modern cold-climate units carry the load down to -15C or -25C without help, so backup is real insurance, not a daily crutch.

## What you are actually buying

When the quote lands, you are paying for four things. The outdoor condenser unit, which contains the compressor and outdoor coil. The indoor unit, which is either a wall-mounted head (ductless) or a coil inside a ducted air handler. The refrigerant lines between them, which have to be vacuum-pumped down to remove moisture before charging. And the labor.

Labor is where the surprises live. The mechanical hardware is the same across most quotes; the difference between a $12k install and a $22k install is mostly time, line-set length, electrical panel work, and whether your contractor eats weather days.

If you remember one sentence from this page, remember this. The unit is a commodity. The install is a craft. You are not choosing a heat pump, you are choosing an installer who happens to sell a heat pump.
