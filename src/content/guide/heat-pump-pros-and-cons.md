Three winters in, having ripped out an oil furnace in 2022 for a Mitsubishi HyperHeat 3-ton multi-zone with dual furnace backup, here is what I have learned about what a heat pump does well, does poorly, and does about the same as the furnace it replaced.

## The pros

Year-round comfort per dollar. Summer cooling matches or beats a separate AC. Winter heating in shoulder season (5C outdoor and above) runs at COP 3 plus. Per-room control with multi-zone. Whisper-quiet operational noise indoor side.

Climate and emissions gains of 60 to 80 percent versus an oil furnace in cold US climates, depending on grid. A COP 2.5 heat pump running on US-average electricity produces 27 percent of the CO2 of an oil furnace per BTU delivered. With a 50 percent solar offset on the household, the emissions approach zero.

Eliminating fossil-fuel infrastructure. No gas meter, no oil tank, no vent pipe through the roof, no CO detector draws. You sell the oil tank for scrap. You cancel the gas utility. You cancel the chimney sweep. Insurance premiums sometimes discount slightly because no combustion appliance sits in the basement.

Federal and state rebates soften the install cost by $4,000 to $12,000 in most US climates. Hands you a modern appliance at the cost of a wall unit.

Reduced annual maintenance cost versus a combustion furnace. No heat exchanger failure, no feed line leaks, no pressure testing certification. Filter cleaning and a yearly refrigerant pressure check are roughly half the maintenance cost of a furnace.

## The cons

Higher upfront cost than a furnace plus AC combo. By $3,000 to $7,000 before rebates. After rebates in good states, clawing back close to even on install.

Lower air delivery temperature. Heat pumps deliver air around 32C to 38C at the indoor outlet. Furnaces deliver 49C to 54C. Vents feel cold to furnace migrants. Adjust within two weeks, hard to adjust for some.

Runtime longer in shoulder season. A heat pump runs low and continuously. Most of the winter the outdoor unit is spinning at low modulation. Some owners find this psychologically discomforting even though it is the COP of how the system works best.

Backup required in cold climates. Cold-climate spec units can hold the house to -25C. Below -25C they call resistance electric backup or dual fuel. Code in cold regions requires backup by law. Backup operation cost is real.

Sensitivity to installer quality. A poorly installed heat pump of the best brand runs worse than a properly installed heat pump of a mediocre brand. DIY refrigeration side is restricted by law. Installer skill is the determinant.

Defrost cycle interrupts every hour or two in cold humid weather for 5 to 10 minutes. Supply air drops to room temperature during defrost. Some owners find this annoying. Better units restore to full heat almost immediately, less mature units pump cold air for the full 10 minutes.

Hidden cost surprise. Panel upgrade to 200A costs $2,500 to $5,000 if your panel is 100A. Line-set in tight wall cavities adds $500 to $1,500. New home run for 240V dedicated circuit adds $400 to $900. Plus $200 for condensate pump.

## Neutral observations

House stays cool and quiet in summer. The unit modulating low and long during cooling is comfortable. Quick recovery when you come home to a hot house, but the unit ramps slowly by design.

Outdoor unit operational noise is what to know. 25 to 50 dB depending on mode and proximity. Some yards hear the unit at 35 dB at 6 feet, similar to a soft conversation.

Per-room comfort control via multi-zone heads has been better than my prior central furnace delivered. Each room holds its own setpoint. Compromise requires a discussion with the spouse, programmer, then 强化 it in the schedule, but in climates that change enough between rooms, the multi-zone approach is a real comfort gain.

## What I would weigh on each side

If your house is built for ducts and you live in the mild south without much cold winter: skip the heat pump and stay with conventional high-efficiency gas plus AC. You save the install complexity and the cost differential with running cost that does not come back. Plus you avoid the divergent operating modes.

If you live in cold climate, holding a furnace in place, and have moderate-to-good electric rate compared to gas rate: dual fuel is the optimal mix. Keep the furnace as backup, run the heat pump for the 320 shoulder days.

If you are building new or doing a deep retrofit: heat-pump all-electric with a strong solar offset is probably the right answer. Emissions trend lowest. Operating cost in some states can drop below dual fuel when solar covers a portion of the runtime.

If you live in cold climate with no gas supply (most of Maine, parts of Vermont, New Hampshire, rural Maritimes): heat pump is the obvious primary heat. The alternative is oil furnace or propane.

## Three lines to take away

The pros are year-round comfort, emissions, and the climate. The cons are run time adjustment, install complexity, and warranty service depending on region.

The actual decision is not about the unit. It is about the installer. Pick the brand with the most credible service network in your area.

Three winters of dual-fuel cold climate work would buy this system again. It runs cheaper than the oil furnace did, the house is warmer in shoulder season, and the maintenance bill is half what oil cost annually. The two-week comfort bump was real for the warm air feel. The total comfort was higher than the furnace actually delivered.
